AI in the Browser

Modern browsers—yes, even those running on mobile phones—are capable of far more than displaying pages and submitting forms.

I built CadenceCam as a small demonstration of that idea. An old phone runs a web app that uses the camera to detect repetitive movement, counts repetitions, and sends the reps signal over WebRTC (wifi only) to a dashboard. The dashboard, running on a separate device, displays the reps and calculates calories. The substantial computation (a model trained through probabilistic learning) happens inside the browser, on the device that captures the movement.

This approach has benefits beyond reducing latency. Camera data can stay on the phone, communication between devices can happen directly over the local network, and the backend can be limited to the jobs that genuinely require it, such as initial pairing and storing workout history. Less data travels to a server, privacy improves, and infrastructure costs remain low.

Not every workload belongs in the frontend. But modern JavaScript engines, and increasingly powerful mobile hardware have moved the boundary considerably. Before adding another backend service, it may be worth asking: could the user’s browser do this work instead?

CadenceCam is my answer to that question—along with a practical way to stop losing count halfway through a workout.

Detecting Repetitive Motion in Video

Point a phone at an exercise machine and ask it a simple question: how many times has that gone round? The obvious way to answer is to recognise the thing that is moving — find the pedal, track it, count its circuits. That is a respectable computer-vision problem, and on a phone it is an expensive one. A model to ship and load, a GPU to keep busy, a battery to flatten, and a fresh way to fail every time someone points the camera at a machine nobody trained it on.

CadenceCam does none of that. It never identifies anything. It does not know what a pedal is, or whether it is watching a bicycle, a leg, a handle or a metronome. It knows only that something in front of the camera keeps coming back, and it counts how often.

That reframing is the entire design. What is in this picture? is a hard question. Is anything in this picture periodic, and at what rate? is a signal-processing question, and signal processing has had a century to get good at it.

One number instead of a million

A 1080p frame is about two million pixels. Ten frames a second is twenty million pixel reads a second, which is not a reasonable thing to ask of a phone that is also meant to run for an hour on a battery.

So the first step throws nearly all of it away. Each frame is drawn into a deliberately tiny offscreen canvas — a budget of roughly 2,500 pixels, with the aspect ratio preserved, so a 16:9 frame lands at about 66 × 38. The browser does that downscale in hardware, for free, and the result is averaged rather than examined. Nothing downstream needs detail. It needs only to know how bright a region is, and how that brightness moves over time.

Brightness here means Rec. 709 luma: the weighted sum the eye would agree with, rather than a flat average of the three channels, so a green object crossing a blue background registers as the change it visibly is instead of washing out.

sq [n] = 1 |Qq| ∑ p∈Qq ( 0.2126Rp +0.7152Gp +0.0722Bp )

Two million numbers per frame have become four: one per region, on the same 0–255 scale as the pixels they came from. Everything after this point is arithmetic on four slow time series, and that is cheap enough to do in a browser tab on a phone old enough to have been retired to a drawer.

Why four quadrants

The natural first guess is one number for the whole frame. It does not work, and it fails in an instructive way: a whole-frame average is often perfectly constant while a great deal is moving inside it. An object that leaves the left half exactly as it enters the right half changes the mean not at all. The motion is still there; averaging has cancelled it.

The opposite extreme — a fine grid of cells — fails for the opposite reason. The smaller a cell, the less of the moving object it ever contains, so the signal in any one cell shrinks while the noise does not. You also acquire several hundred channels that disagree with each other, and now have to decide which of them to believe.

Four is the smallest split that breaks the cancellation along both axes while leaving each region large enough to carry a strong signal. The quadrants are numbered the way mathematics numbers them, counter-clockwise from the top right:

Q2Q1
Q3Q4

The app draws those dividing lines over the live preview, which turns an abstraction into a framing instruction: put the moving part where it will cross a line.

Three things in that picture are worth more than a paragraph of theory.

It is not a sine wave. It is a plateau, interrupted. The quadrant sits at its background brightness until the moving object crosses it, drops steeply, holds briefly at the bottom and recovers. A cycle is one notch, not one oscillation, and the notch is narrow compared to the period.

The amplitudes are small. The plateau sits around 150 and the dips reach 136 — a swing of fourteen units out of a possible 255. On a worse-lit machine it is single digits. Anything that moves the whole frame by a comparable amount is, to this detector, indistinguishable from exercise.

And the four channels disagree. They have different amplitudes, because the object crosses them to different depths, and different phase, because it reaches them at different moments. The one thing they share is the period. Everything the detector later does to decide whether to believe itself is built on that single fact.

Ten samples a second

Human exercise cadence lives in a narrow band. Thirty repetitions a minute is a slow, deliberate grind; a hundred and twenty is a sprint. That is 0.5 to 2 Hz, and committing to it early is what makes everything downstream tractable.

Sampling theory sets the floor. To represent a 2 Hz component at all, the sampling rate must exceed twice it:

fs > 2fmax =4 Hz , chosen: fs=10 Hz

Ten hertz is comfortably above the floor without being wasteful. It puts the top of the band at two fifths of the Nyquist limit, where a gentle filter still has room to work, and it quantises the position of a detected peak to ±50 ms — about five per cent of a cycle at one hertz, which is fine for a count and fine for a pace.

Sampling at the camera's own frame rate would cost three to six times as much for information the filter is about to discard. So the sampler runs on its own timer, at its own rate, and simply takes whatever frame is current.

Separating the rhythm from everything else

Two problems stand between those raw traces and a count.

The first is level. A quadrant's brightness sits around 120 while a repetition moves it by ten. The interesting part of the signal is a few per cent of the number carrying it.

The second is everything else in the room. Someone walking past, the sun going behind a cloud, the camera adjusting itself, a flickering light — all of it lands in exactly the same trace as the exercise.

Both are addressed by committing to the band. First the window is centred by subtracting its own mean, which removes the resting level outright; without that step a filter starting from rest sees a brightness of 120 as a step input and rings on it hard enough to swamp a repetition of ten.

xq[n] = sq[n] − 1N ∑ k=0 N−1 sq[k]

Then a band-pass. One second-order IIR section — a biquad — applied as a difference equation, one sample at a time:

y[n] = b0x[n] +b1x[n−1] +b2x[n−2] −a1y[n−1] −a2y[n−2]

which in the z-domain is the ratio of two quadratics:

H(z) = b0+b1z−1+b2z−2 1+a1z−1+a2z−2

The coefficients come from Robert Bristow-Johnson's Audio EQ Cookbook — the same formulae the Web Audio API's own filter nodes are built on. For a band-pass with constant peak gain, with the centre frequency placed at the geometric mean of the two edges and the quality factor following from the width:

f0= fLfH =1 Hz , Q= f0 fH−fL =11.5≈0.67
ω0= 2πf0fs , α= sinω02Q , a0=1+α
b0=αa0 , b1=0 , b2=−αa0 a1=−2cosω0a0 , a2=1−αa0

One section is enough. A Q of 0.67 is a wide, gentle band — which is what is wanted, because the cadence is not known in advance and a sharp filter would simply be a way of rejecting half the users. At 10 Hz the Nyquist limit is 5 Hz, comfortably more than an octave above the top of the band, so there is nothing up there that needs a steeper skirt.

The filter is re-run from rest over the whole fifteen-second window on every new sample, rather than carrying its state forward. That costs a few thousand multiply-adds a second — nothing next to capturing the frames in the first place — and it buys a useful guarantee: what the graph shows and what the counter counts are the same function of the same samples, and cannot quietly drift apart. The first two seconds of each filtered window are discarded while the filter settles, because a band-pass starting from rest rings on the arrival of the signal, and that ringing would otherwise dominate the very statistics used to decide what counts as a repetition.

From a waveform to a count

It is tempting to reach for a Fourier transform here, or for autocorrelation. Both will tell you the dominant frequency of the window, confidently and accurately, and neither will tell you what you need.

A count is not a frequency. A count needs events: this repetition happened, now, add one. A frequency estimate integrated over time is a guess about how many events probably occurred, and a guess that drifts by two per cent will be wrong by a repetition every fifty — a counter that is quietly, plausibly wrong is worse than one that admits it cannot see. Sets also start and stop, and a window that is half exercise and half rest has a perfectly good dominant frequency that corresponds to nothing.

So the detector looks for peaks, and judges each one on two properties.

Prominence — how far a local maximum stands above the higher of the two valleys flanking it. Walk outwards in both directions until the signal rises back to the peak's own height, note the lowest point reached on each side, and take the shallower of the two:

P(i) = y[i] − max ( minj∈L y[j] , minj∈R y[j] )

Prominence is used instead of an absolute height because the baseline moves and the height does not mean anything on its own. It is the same measure used to decide whether a hill is a mountain in its own right or merely a shoulder of the one next to it, and the same measure used to find heartbeats in a photoplethysmogram — a problem that sits in a similar frequency range and has precisely the same difficulty.

The threshold a peak must clear has two terms:

θ= max ( 4.0, 1.5⋅ 1N ∑ n=0 N−1 y[n]2 )

The relative term scales with how active the window is, so a brightly lit machine with a strong signal does not accept the small wobbles that a dim one would have to. The absolute floor exists because a relative threshold on its own is self-defeating: show it a window containing nothing but noise, and it will faithfully set a threshold that the noise clears.

That floor is not set by sensor noise. Per-pixel noise averages away almost entirely across the several hundred pixels in a quadrant. What survives averaging is everything that moves the whole frame at once — auto-exposure hunting, mains flicker, the camera's own denoising — and that is a far larger number than the sensor's.

Distance gives the second rule. Two repetitions cannot fall closer together than the period of the fastest cadence the band admits, so accepted peaks must be at least five samples apart:

Δn≥ fsfmax = 10 Hz2 Hz =5samples

Where two candidates fall closer than that, the more prominent survives. Resolving by prominence rather than by position keeps the outcome independent of which end of the window the scan started from.

When one cycle has two peaks

Real signals are not as obliging as the clean trace above. The most common complication, by a wide margin, is that one repetition produces more than one peak.

This is geometry, not mathematics. A quadrant boundary is a line, and a real mechanism crosses it more than once per cycle: a foot and the pedal under it, a limb and its shadow, the near and the far side of a flywheel. Each crossing is a genuine brightness event. Only one of them is a repetition.

When the secondary event is much smaller, prominence disposes of it without further help. That is the honest limit of looking at one channel. The way past it is to stop.

Agreement as evidence

The detector analyses all four quadrants independently and then asks a different question: do any two of them agree about the period?

|Ta−Tb| max(Ta,Tb) ≤0.25

Two, deliberately, and not a majority. A movement may only ever cross two quadrants — a pedal low on the left of the frame need never trouble the top right — so requiring three or four would reject perfectly good framing. The period of each channel is taken as the median interval between its own peaks, so a single missed or spurious peak moves it hardly at all.

Note what is not required: phase. The quadrants see the same movement at different moments, and sometimes in opposition — here the green and orange traces are very nearly anti-phase, which is exactly what two neighbouring quadrants look like when an object leaves one as it enters the other.

That is also why the peaks of agreeing channels are never merged into one train. They carry the same period at different phases, and fusing them would smear the very timing the count depends on. Instead, each channel keeps its own tally, counted entirely from its own peaks, and a tally is believed when a second channel has independently arrived within two repetitions of it. A channel that has locked onto its own noise reaches a number nothing else is near, and that is what gives it away — not its signal strength, which is no help at all, but the size of its claim.

Three further conditions have to hold before anything is counted.

The movement must be sustained: at least five peaks in a row, none further from the last than the period of the slowest cadence in the band. One peak, or two, is somebody shifting in a chair or reaching for a towel. Until the fifth arrives nothing is counted at all — and when it does, all five count together, because each is evidence for the others and none of them was a repetition on its own.

The movement must come back. A repetition is a round trip: the object enters the quadrant and leaves it again, so the brightness that fell returns to where it was. Putting a dumbbell down is not a round trip — the object goes and stays gone — but to a band-pass the two are identical, because a filter blind to a constant treats rose and stayed exactly like rose and fell. So that test is made on the quadrant's raw brightness, where the distinction still exists.

And a peak must stand up against the recent ones in its own channel, rather than only against the window's average activity. When a set ends and the movement slides out of the window, the relative threshold decays after it, and the camera's own speckle is then perfectly capable of clearing a threshold set by the camera's own speckle.

Finally, the detector says which of these it is in — borrowing the vocabulary of a phase-locked loop, it is acquiring while the buffer fills, searching when no two channels agree, and locked when they do. Only in the last of those does the number on screen mean anything, and the app says so rather than showing a confident figure it has no business being confident about.

What the method is good at

It needs nothing from the machine. No sensor, no magnet, no bracket, no Bluetooth pairing, no cooperation from a manufacturer who stopped supporting the model in 2019. If it moves in a cycle and the camera can see it, it can be counted — a bike, a stepper, a rowing machine, an arm crank, a limb with no machine at all.

It is cheap. About 2,500 pixel reads ten times a second, a two-pole filter over a 150-sample window, and a peak scan over the same window. That is a rounding error on any device with a camera, which is what makes it reasonable to run continuously, in a browser tab, on a phone that is otherwise retired.

The video never goes anywhere. Not as a privacy policy — as an architecture. The only thing that leaves the sampling stage is four numbers per frame, and they are already an average over several hundred pixels. There is no frame to transmit, store or leak, because by the time anything is counted the frames have been discarded.

It fails honestly. The lock state, the corroboration requirement and a count that only ever rises are all aimed at one particular kind of failure: a count that stalls and says it is searching, rather than one that runs away. A detector that stops is a nuisance. A detector that confidently invents forty repetitions is a liar, and much harder to notice.

Where the method runs out

It needs contrast, and it needs a crossing. The signal is brightness difference. A dark machine in a dark room, or a movement that stays entirely within one quadrant without ever crossing a boundary, produces a trace with nothing in it. Framing is not a detail here; it is half the instrument.

The camera fights back. This is the most irritating one. A phone camera is not a passive sensor — it runs its own exposure and white-balance loops, and when a dark object repeatedly enters the frame, the camera helpfully brightens everything to compensate. The band is a commitment. Anything slower than 0.5 Hz or faster than 2 Hz is invisible by construction. That is a deliberate trade — the band is what makes the filter, the sampling rate, the minimum distance and the gap rule all derivable from one decision — but it does mean very slow, deliberate movements are outside the design.

It counts crossings, not exercise. The detector has no idea what it is watching. A ceiling fan, a pendulum, a washing machine or someone pacing back and forth across the frame are all, to it, perfectly good repetitions. The agreement and sustain rules make accidental counting unlikely, not impossible.

It is always slightly behind. A peak cannot be confirmed until the signal has turned back down after it and come back far enough to prove it was a round trip, which costs about one cadence period — a little over a second at a typical pace. And the first lock of a session cannot happen until the fifteen-second window has filled. Nothing is lost — the repetition is counted — but the number on screen always belongs to a moment slightly in the past.

What comes next

Every threshold quoted above — the prominence floor of four, the factor of one and a half, the fifteen-second window, the tolerance of a quarter, the requirement that a tally be corroborated within two — is a number, and every number is a decision that could have been made differently and worse.

None of them were argued into place. They were measured, against hundreds of recorded signals captured from real machines and from a bench rig built for the purpose, and several of them are not what the original reasoning suggested.

The articles that follow take that apart from three directions. The first is the software: how an application that has to run this loop ten times a second in a browser, mirror it to a second device over a direct peer connection, and survive being written by one person in their spare time, is actually put together. The second is testing: how you write automated tests for something whose input is the physical world, what a remote-controlled bench rig buys you, and which failures only ever turn up when a human points a real phone at a real machine. The third is the one that changed the most minds — what happened when every tuning decision was replayed against a library of recorded signals, and how a detector that counted about right became one that counts exactly.

Fast, Yet Cheap

The previous article was about a detector: four brightness signals, a band-pass filter, and a rule for deciding that a bump in a noisy line is a repetition. This one is about the application that has to run it, and about the two numbers that shaped nearly every decision in it.

The first number is a hundred milliseconds. The detector takes a sample ten times a second, and on every sample it re-analyses the last fifteen seconds of all four signals. Miss the deadline and you do not get a slightly late answer; you get a gap in the signal, which is a different signal, which is a different count.

The second number is zero. Nobody is paid to run CadenceCam and nothing it does earns anything back. It is one person's project, written in the evenings, deployed from a laptop, and it has to stay inside Google Cloud's free allowances — not as a stretch goal, but as a condition of existing at all.

Those two budgets normally pull in opposite directions. Fast usually means more machine, and more machine is exactly what a free allowance does not contain. Here they happen to agree, because of one decision made early and never seriously revisited: do the arithmetic where the data already is. The frames are in the browser. The browser has a processor. Everything else follows from refusing to move the data somewhere more expensive.

One language on both sides of the wire

CadenceCam is Scala 3 from end to end. The browser application is Scala.js — the same compiler, the same language, emitting JavaScript instead of bytecode — with Laminar for the interface. The server is Scala on the JVM, with ZIO HTTP for the routes and Firestore for storage. Both are built by one sbt build from one repository, on one compiler version.

The sizes are a fair summary of where the work is. The browser application is about eight thousand lines of Scala. The server is about five thousand. And between them sits a third project, compiled twice — once to bytecode and once to JavaScript — that is six hundred and fifty lines long.

That small project is the entire contract between the two halves: thirty-four shapes that cross the wire, and the fifteen route paths they travel on. The path is not a string in the frontend that happens to match a string in the backend. It is a constant that lives in the companion object of the type that travels on it. Every call the browser makes is built from that constant, and a test on the server asserts that the route the server actually declared is the same one. Change the value and the server's suite fails; rename it and the browser stops compiling. Either way the mismatch turns up on a laptop, rather than as a 404 on a phone, in a gym, with no console open.

The codecs are derived from the same definitions, so nothing describes a payload twice. There is no schema file, no generator step, no second description that can fall behind the first. A field added to a message is added once, and both ends are looking at it in the same instant; there is no state in which one of them knows about a field and the other does not.

The reason this matters is not that it saves typing. It is that the boundary between client and server is, in most projects, the one place where nothing checks anything — where a typo survives until a human finds it. For a project whose entire budget is one person's evenings, the bug you genuinely cannot afford is the one that takes an evening to locate.

There is a second dividend, and it turned out to be the larger one. The detector is ordinary Scala: no model, no GPU kernel, no library compiled from another language. Code that is ordinary Scala can be taken out of the browser and run anywhere Scala runs — including against hundreds of recorded signals, offline, as many times as a question needs asking. That is the subject of the last article in this series, and it would not have been possible if the detector had been written in the browser's own terms.

A build with no JavaScript in it

There is no package.json anywhere in the repository. No bundler, no node_modules, no separate frontend pipeline, no second thing to deploy.

The Scala.js linker runs as a resource generator for the backend. Its output, main.js, is written into the server's managed resources alongside the hand-written index.html, the stylesheet, the web-app manifest, the icons and the legal documents. The server's own JAR therefore carries the browser application inside it. One build produces one artifact, that artifact becomes one Docker image, and one gcloud run deploy ships the client and the server together or not at all. There is no window in which a new frontend is talking to an old backend, because there is no way to deploy one without the other.

One small thing happens to the bundle on the way through. The build prepends a single line that records the version and build time into the browser's own storage, so the About panel can display the build the browser is actually running next to the build the server believes it is serving. The comment in the build file explains why it exists, and it is not a hypothetical: a browser holding a cached bundle is indistinguishable from one that has picked up the latest, and hours of test results have been read as detector behaviour when they were an old build on one phone.

The server never watches the exercise

Here is the division of labour, which is the whole architecture in one table.

The left column runs at ten hertz. Four quadrants at ten samples a second is forty numbers a second, each one distilled from about two and a half thousand pixel reads, and each one triggering a fresh pass of the filter and the peak detector over the last fifteen seconds of all four signals.

The right column runs at a tenth of a hertz. While a workout is in progress, the counting device sends the server three numbers — the repetition count, an accumulator the dashboard needs for frequency-based calories, and how long the set has been running — once every ten seconds.

Consider the alternatives honestly. Uploading video is megabits per second per user, a bill that scales with the number of people exercising, and it moves the detector's hundred-millisecond deadline to the far end of a round trip. Uploading just the four brightness signals is far cheaper — but it is still forty values a second instead of three every ten, and the analysis then has to run somewhere that is being billed for it. Neither buys a single thing the browser was not already able to do.

Three separate properties fall out of the same decision. It is cheap, because the traffic scales with workouts rather than with repetitions. It is fast, because the count appears the moment the camera sees the movement, with no round trip between the two. And it is private in a way a policy document cannot be: there is no upload path for the picture, so there is nothing to promise about what happens to it.

The second screen is not a client of the server

A phone propped where it can see the machine is a poor display. It is three metres away, face-on to a moving leg, and the person exercising is not going to walk over and squint at it. So a second device — a tablet, a laptop, another phone — shows the count, the pace and the calories while the first one counts.

The obvious way to build that is for the counter to post each reading to the server and the dashboard to poll for it. It is also the worst of the available options. Every reading becomes two requests; the latency is whatever the poll interval is; and the server acquires a job it did not previously have, which is holding the live state of a workout in progress.

What actually happens is that the two browsers talk to each other. A WebRTC data channel is opened directly between the counting device and each watching device, and the readings travel across it. The server's only role is the introduction: an offer, an answer and a handful of connection candidates, filed under the account in Firestore and handed to whichever end is waiting for them. Once the channel is open the server is not part of the workout at all.

The ICE server list is empty — no STUN server, no TURN relay. The two devices are expected to be on the same network, which is the only arrangement that makes physical sense for a phone on a tripod and a tablet on the handlebars, and it means the readings never reach the public internet, let alone a relay somebody has to pay for.

The decisive argument for this was not cost, though. A live room held in one server process requires both devices to land in that same process, and nothing about how a platform scales will guarantee that. The deployment happens to be capped at a single instance today — but a design that depends on that cap is a design that can never be uncapped. Two browsers that have exchanged candidates do not care which instance introduced them, or whether it is still running.

What is left on the server is a post box, and it is polled at rates chosen to be boring: every three seconds while a counter watches for a dashboard that may have appeared, every half second while a link is actually being negotiated, and not at all on the watching side once its channel is open. Readings are sent when what the counter would say changes, not on a timer. Connection diagnostics are filed in a different place from the exchange itself, because one diagnostic per connection-state change, against a single offer and a single answer, will otherwise bury the two messages that pairing actually depends on.

Who owns the arithmetic

The counter sends a measurement. It does not send a conclusion.

What crosses the data channel is the repetition count, the frequency accumulator, the elapsed time, the status line and a couple of flags. What does not cross it is calories — because the counting device has no business knowing what anybody weighs. The dashboard holds the account's settings, so the dashboard does the arithmetic: weight, the chosen exercise's factor, and whether that exercise counts by repetitions or by frequency.

Two of the numbers on that screen arrived from the phone: the count and the clock. Everything else on it — the calorie total, both per-minute rates, both half-hour projections — was worked out by the device displaying it, out of that measurement and the settings below.

None of that is known to the counting device, and none of it needs to be. Choosing a different exercise re-renders a dashboard; it does not involve renegotiating anything with a phone across the room. When a dashboard asks for a different exercise, the identifier travels to the counter as an opaque string which the counter merely repeats to everything else watching — it never looks up what the string means, and it could not calculate a calorie if it wanted to.

One detail of the dashboard is a deliberate inversion of the usual rule. The status line along the bottom — Counting · 1.1s/rep — is sent across the channel as finished text, not as a state for the dashboard to render in its own words. The two screens are supposed to read identically, and the only way to guarantee that is to let one of them do the wording and have the other repeat it.

Scale to zero, and the heartbeat that goes with it

The server runs on Cloud Run, which starts a container when a request arrives and bills for the time it spends serving. When nobody is exercising there is no container and there is no bill. For an application with one regular user and a few test devices, "nobody is exercising" describes almost the entire week.

That model has one awkward interaction with a workout, which is a long-lived thing happening on a platform that has no concept of one. The ten-second progress report answers both halves of it. It keeps the account's record roughly current — the record that a dashboard's Stop turns into a history row, and the mark whose age decides when an abandoned set should be closed. And the traffic itself is what stops the instance being reclaimed while somebody is mid-set. Unchanged totals are reported too, deliberately: reporting only when the count changes would go silent during a rest, which is precisely the moment a counting session must not quietly evaporate.

Nothing live depends on that record, which is the point. A counter that is reloaded in the middle of a set resumes from the browser's own storage, and a dashboard gets its count over the data channel. The server's copy is the history, not the backup.

The same thinking removed the last background job. A workout whose most recent report is more than ten minutes old is closed as idle — not by a scheduler, not by a cron job, not by a timer in some process, but by whichever request next happens to look at that account. There is nothing to keep alive in order to have something expire, which is the only kind of expiry that is free on a platform that scales to zero.

A backend that is added to, not edited

The server's routes are not registered in Main. At startup, the backend scans its own classpath for objects implementing a small plugin interface and finds twenty-three of them: login for three identity providers, session refresh, logout, the account's settings, workout control, workout history, the progress reports, the peer post box, trace capture, the bench's test events, and so on. Each one declares an identifier, its routes, and the capabilities it needs — Firestore, the session store, the HTTP client — as a value the loader resolves against what the host actually has.

A plugin whose capabilities are missing is skipped and reported, not fatal. A duplicate identifier, a conflicting route, an incompatible interface version or an exception during initialisation isolates that plugin and leaves the rest of the server running. The optional diagnostic plugin is a separate JAR that is only on the classpath when the developer's own configuration enables it, so a facility that should not exist in a deployment is genuinely absent from it rather than merely switched off inside it.

This is not team-scale machinery; there is no team. It is a hedge against the only reviewer this code will ever get, which is the same person six months later. Adding a feature should not require remembering to edit the file that starts the server, because that registration line is exactly the thing that gets forgotten.

The static files follow the same instinct. There is no file server and no document root. There is a list of names — the index, the stylesheet, the bundle, its source map, the manifest, the icons, and the four public documents — each mapped to a route that hands back a classpath resource. A path that is not on the list cannot be served, which is a far smaller thing to reason about than a directory.

What the free tier actually absorbs

It is worth putting the numbers next to each other.

While a set is running, the counting device makes roughly forty-six requests a minute: twenty checks that it is still the device holding the counting role, twenty polls of the signalling post box for a dashboard that might have appeared, and six progress reports. A half-hour workout is therefore something like fourteen hundred requests and a hundred and eighty small writes. At rest, an account is one document, plus one subdocument per workout kept as history, plus a few signalling rows that the next offer prunes behind it.

Note what that breakdown says. Forty of those forty-six requests a minute are housekeeping — am I still the counter, has anyone come to watch — and they happen whether or not a single repetition is being performed. A counter sitting idle with its screen open is very nearly as chatty as one in the middle of a set. What the exercise itself contributes is six requests a minute, which is the clearest possible statement of where this application's work is not happening.

Cloud Run's free allowance is a monthly quantity of request time and memory; Firestore's is a daily quantity of document reads and writes. One person's workouts and a handful of test devices fit inside both with room to spare, and the project has so far run entirely within them.

The honest reason it is free is that there is one user. But the shape is the part that would survive more of them. Nothing the server does scales with repetitions. The forty numbers a second — the part that genuinely scales with how much exercise is happening in the world — never becomes traffic at all. It stays on a device that somebody else already bought, already charges, and is already holding.

Where it would stop being cheap

Three places, and it is worth being specific about them.

The first page load is enormous. The deployed main.js is 8,252,441 bytes, served uncompressed, with a cache lifetime of zero. The size is the output of Scala.js's fast linker rather than its optimising one — the quicker link and the fatter bundle. The expiry is zero on purpose, and the build says why: every deployment is still a test deployment, and a cached stylesheet outliving a server restart hides a change that was in fact shipped. Each is a line of build configuration away from being otherwise. For now the price is paid on every page load, by a developer, on a home network — and it would be the first thing to change on the day a stranger opened the app on mobile data.

One instance is a ceiling, not a floor. The service is deployed with a maximum of one instance. It bounds what a burst of traffic can start, which is a reasonable thing to want when the bill is personal — but it also means that real concurrent load would queue rather than scale. Nothing in the design depends on the cap any more, now that every request reads its state from Firestore and the live path is peer-to-peer, which means raising it is a flag rather than a rewrite. And a container that has scaled to zero still has to start a JVM before it can answer, so the first request after a quiet night pays for the privilege of being free all night.

The detector is written twice. One language on both sides of the wire is a real saving, and it stops at the edge of the browser. The native Android and iOS Counter is a Flutter application — forty-six hundred lines of Dart — with its own ten-hertz four-quadrant acquisition and its own copy of the detector. A second implementation of the one algorithm is exactly what a shared project exists to prevent, and every tuning decision now has to land in both of them. That is the real price of a native client, and it is paid in the only currency this project is ever short of.

Fine-Tuning

A detector that watches the world has numbers in it, and somebody has to choose them. How far must a peak stand above its surroundings before it is a repetition rather than a ripple? How long a stretch of signal should the decision be made over? How much may two quadrants of the same frame disagree before one of them is lying?

There are fifteen such numbers in this one, and every one of them started life as an argument. A plausible argument, in most cases, made from how a camera ought to behave and how a person ought to exercise. The trouble with a plausible argument is that it produces a value with no error bar and no way to tell whether it is right — and when the thing being tuned is a filter watching a limb move through a patch of light, the arguments turn out to be wrong in ways nobody would have guessed, and wrong in a different direction each time.

This article is about what happened when the arguments were replaced with recordings. It is the last of four, and the one the other three were building towards: a library of real signals, captured from real phones watching real movement, against which a proposed change to any of those fifteen numbers can be replayed as many times as the question needs asking.

Fifteen numbers in one place

Almost everything the detector can be tuned by lives in a single immutable record called DetectorSettings. It is an ordinary Scala case class with fifteen fields and a default for each, and it exists in that shape deliberately — its own comment says it is gathered in one place so it can be settled against recordings rather than argued about. One more number, how long a stretch of signal the analysis runs over, lives next door with the buffer that holds it, and it has a section to itself below.

The fields are the whole of the detector's behaviour. The sampling rate, ten hertz. The band-pass edges, half a hertz to two. The closest two repetitions may fall, five samples. The prominence threshold, which is a floor of four units or one and a half times the window's own activity, whichever is larger. How much signal must accumulate before a cadence may be claimed, a hundred and fifty samples. How closely two quadrants' periods must match, a quarter. How many peaks in a row make a sustained movement, five. How far a quadrant's tally may exceed its neighbour's and still be believed, two. How far a peak's own quadrant must come back down afterwards, a fifth of how far the others did. How tall a peak must stand against the repetitions before it, a half. How far back before it reaches, ten repetitions. How many recent repetitions the reported pace is averaged over, ten. And twenty samples dropped at the front of every window while the filter settles.

The most important line in that file is the one that admits what is not known. The record's comment reads: the values began as reasoning; several have since been measured against recorded signals, and where one has, its comment says against how many recordings and what the change did; the rest are still the reasoned value. That distinction is maintained field by field. Four of the fifteen now name the pool of recordings they were replayed against and what moved when they changed; a fifth rests on a measurement of its own. The other ten still say, in effect, this seemed sensible and nothing has contradicted it yet.

That is a less satisfying state of affairs than a detector where every constant is justified, and it is a far more honest one. A reader of the code can tell at a glance which numbers are load-bearing evidence and which are placeholders that have simply never been challenged, and so can the person who wrote them, a year later, at the point of wanting to change one.

What a recording is

The counter can capture what it is seeing. The control sits in the menu on the counting phone, and the bench rig described in the previous article presses it automatically after every test, so a full suite leaves six recordings behind without anyone touching the handset.

What gets sent is deliberately blunt: a JSON document holding the sample rate, four named series of up to three thousand six hundred numbers each — six minutes of quadrant brightness at ten hertz — the count the detector had reached, the lock state written out in words, an optional note, what the camera reported it was capable of, what became of the attempt to hold its exposure still and at which sample that settled, and which handset recorded it.

Each of those fields is there for a stated reason. The lock state is in words rather than inferred afterwards, so a replay knows what the detector believed at the time rather than what a rebuilt detector would now believe. The camera's capabilities are carried because they differ by device in ways that decide whether the controls can be held still at all, and there is no other way to read them off a phone. The control outcome sits in the same record as the signal it may have disturbed, so the two can be read against each other instead of against a wall-clock timestamp and a hopeful memory of when the picture changed. And the device is named because two handsets pointed at the same panel have produced recordings that count quite differently, and nothing else in a trace says which was which.

The buffer is taken whole rather than from a chosen window, for the reason that makes recordings useful at all: what makes a trace worth keeping is usually something that already happened, and the interesting part is often just before the moment somebody reached for the button.

The replay, and the control it needs

None of this would be possible if the detector were a stream. It is not. The band-pass filter runs over the whole buffer from rest on every sample rather than carrying state between calls, which keeps its output a pure function of the samples; the analysis of a channel is a function of that channel's samples, its quadrant, and a settings record; and the settings record is immutable with a default for every field. Replaying a recording against a changed detector is therefore a matter of constructing DetectorSettings(prominenceFloor = 4.0) and running the same code over stored numbers.

That is the whole trick, and it is not a trick at all — it is what ordinary code buys you. There is no model to retrain, no inference server, no checkpoint. There are a few hundred lines of signal processing with their parameters lifted into one record, which means the expensive part of answering "what if the floor were four?" is reading the recordings, not rebuilding the system.

The expensive part of answering it correctly is something else: the control. Every candidate change is replayed across the whole pool with current behaviour run alongside it, and the rule is that the control must reproduce the counts the phones actually reported at the time. A control that does not is a sweep that means nothing, however good the candidate's numbers look — and a control that had quietly stopped reproducing them has invalidated whole sweeps on this project. The recordings are evidence; a replay is only evidence if the unchanged detector still agrees with what the unchanged detector did.

The hundred-and-first repetition

Every bench test run with a dark object on a light background came in exactly one over. A hundred repetitions performed, a hundred and one counted, and the extra one arrived at the instant the object left the frame at the end of the set.

The cause is a property of the filter, and it is worth stating slowly because it is the clearest example in the project of a correct component producing a wrong answer. A band-pass filter is blind to a constant. A level that rises and stays and a level that rises and falls back both decay to zero in it at the same rate, because the filter has no way to express the difference. The peak detector then looks for local maxima in what the filter produced, and finds one at the departure.

Which way the error points depends on the contrast. With a light object on a dark ground, the object leaving is a trough, and a trough at the end does no harm — it gives the last real peak the footing it needs. With a dark object on a light ground, the object leaving is a peak of its own, and it gets counted.

Eighty-four samples of one quadrant of one recording, kept in the test suite as a fixture. A dark disc circles on a light ground; the quadrant sits near 218 and drops to about 191 each time the disc passes through it. The last of those passes is just before six seconds; after it the brightness returns to its resting level and simply stays there. Below, the project's own band-pass applied to those same numbers. Six local maxima, one for each time the brightness rose: five of them are followed by the disc passing again, and the sixth is not. It measures 10.3 where its five neighbours measure 11.7, 11.8, 12.7, 13.6 and 14.5. No threshold on height, prominence or shape separates it from them.

The fix is to stop asking the filter. A repetition is a round trip — the object enters a quadrant and leaves it again, so the brightness that moved comes back within a period — while a weight being put down is a departure with no return. That distinction survives in the quadrant's own raw brightness, where the filter has not yet erased it. A peak is counted only if the level falls back within the slowest period the band admits, by at least a fifth of how far the other peaks in that window fell.

A fifth, specifically, because of what twenty-four recorded tests said: every real repetition came back at least a third as far as the typical one, and every phantom less than a seventh. A fifth sits between them, half again above the worst phantom and a third below the weakest honest repetition. Of the four peaks the fixture above puts to that test, the three real ones fall back by 28.6, 28.4 and 28.0 brightness units, and the departure by 1.9.

The threshold is stated as a share of the movement's own excursion rather than as an absolute, for the same reason the prominence threshold is adaptive: how far a repetition falls depends on contrast, framing and distance, none of which are known in advance, while this peak measured against the others is comparable everywhere.

Replayed over those recordings, the six suites run since the bench began holding the figure still before each test went from four correct in twelve to ten, with nothing that had counted correctly now counting wrong. The cost is not a repetition but a delay: the newest peak cannot be judged until the movement has had time to come back, so the last repetition of a set is credited up to two seconds later, inside the pause that follows it.

A threshold carrying a minute of history

The threshold a peak must clear is the greater of a fixed floor and a multiple of how active the surrounding window is. The adaptive half is what lets the detector work in a dim room and a bright one without being told which it is in. It is also, if the window is long, a way of judging the present against the past.

That window used to be a minute. Two consequences were measured, and neither had been predicted. The last repetition of a set waited twenty to thirty seconds to be counted, because the threshold had been set by the set that preceded it and the movement had to age out of the window before the final peak could clear it. And a test run immediately after a stronger one spent its first half-minute failing to clear a threshold that belonged to the previous test.

Fifteen seconds instead. Against the same recordings, the final delay measured at seven and eleven seconds, with the counts unchanged at exactly a hundred.

What makes this more than a number being lowered is the floor underneath it. The window cannot shrink freely: nothing is counted until five peaks have accumulated, and after the filter's settling samples are dropped, fifteen seconds holds about six cycles of the slowest cadence the band admits. Shortening it further would start rejecting slow exercise rather than stale history. A test now asserts that the window is wide enough for the sustained-movement rule, so the two cannot drift apart as one or the other is tuned.

Two tallies, and a second opinion

This one took several attempts, and the ones that failed are the instructive part.

A movement crosses two quadrants of the frame, and the detector locks onto the pair whose periods agree. Which of the two leads the count is settled by signal power, often by a couple of per cent — and the one that loses has repeatedly been the one that saw every repetition. Six suites in a row came in one short that way.

The obvious repair is to fuse the two peak trains into one. That was tried twice and thrown away twice. The quadrants carry the same period at different phases, so fusing them means estimating the phase between them, and the estimate is never clean enough: one attempt reached a hundred and twenty repetitions on a hundred-repetition test, and the more cautious one fixed four recordings and broke four.

What worked was to stop fusing anything. Each agreeing quadrant keeps its own tally entirely from its own peaks, and the count is the largest of them. Largest rather than an average or a vote, because the error runs one way only: a quadrant that does not really see the movement finds fewer crossings, not more. Measured over fifty-nine recordings, the partner ran as much as a hundred repetitions below the leader and never more than two above. Taking the larger turned no correct count into a wrong one, and took the number of recordings counted exactly right from nineteen to thirty-six.

Then the bound that makes the largest safe. A quadrant the movement barely reaches has almost no signal; its threshold falls to the fixed floor, and the noise it then finds can match the cadence by chance. On real recordings such a channel reported a hundred and thirty-six where a hundred repetitions were performed. Guarding on signal power was tried at two thresholds and let every one of them through — the noise was not weak, it was simply not the movement. What gives it away is the size of its claim. Two quadrants watching one movement disagree about the edges of a set and never about its middle, by one repetition and at most two, so a tally is believed only when another channel arrived within two of it.

Comparing against the leader instead, which was the first version of this, failed in both directions: it believed a runaway whenever the runaway happened to be leading, since nothing bounds a leader against itself, and it could freeze the count outright when a channel that started agreeing late won on power from a tally of zero. A recorded suite contains exactly that — a test that counted nothing for two minutes while reporting the previous test's total, and passed, because the reference climbed to meet a number that was never measured.

Over sixty-five recordings from four handsets, tallying every agreeing quadrant and bounding it by corroboration took forty-one counted exactly right to forty-seven, with nothing correct made wrong and no runaway totals.

The speckle that counted after the set

The adaptive threshold has a failure mode at the other end of a set, and it is the one that reads worst to a user: the count carries on rising after the exercise has stopped.

The arithmetic is unavoidable once stated. The adaptive half of the threshold is one and a half times the root-mean-square of the window, and noise peaks routinely exceed one and a half times their own RMS. So a window holding nothing but noise sets a threshold that noise clears. When a set ends and the movement slides out of the window, the threshold decays after it, and the camera's own speckle starts counting. One recording gained eleven repetitions that way, and another seven.

The fix measures a peak against the tallest thing in the preceding stretch of its own channel. Within a set, that is another repetition, so every repetition passes. After a set, it is still a repetition, so speckle does not. Both bounds are measurements: across sixty-four recordings, cool-down noise stood at about a third of the movement that preceded it, and no genuine repetition fell below three quarters of its own set's median. A half sits between, a third clear of the worst honest dip.

The interesting part is what the memory is allowed to learn from. It reads the signal, not the peaks already credited — and that is load-bearing rather than incidental. A memory that learned only from accepted peaks would stop learning the moment it began rejecting; a room that then dimmed would ratchet the threshold out of reach and the count would simply stop. That is a silent, total failure, where the one it replaces is merely wrong. Auto-exposure was measured moving a signal's amplitude by as much as forty per cent in ten seconds, so a room that dims is not a hypothetical.

The memory reaches back ten repetitions rather than a fixed number of seconds, so it scales with cadence — twelve seconds at a slow pace and four at a fast one — and does not judge a repetition against a scene that no longer exists.

Replayed over eighty-eight recordings from five handsets: sixty-one counted exactly right became sixty-four, counts above a hundred fell from fifteen to seven, and the worst count anywhere fell from a hundred and thirty-one to a hundred and one.

The floor that was rejecting first repetitions

The fixed floor under the adaptive threshold was five. It is now four, and the reason is a single recording.

Prominence is measured against the higher of the two valleys flanking a peak. The first repetition of a set has no valley on one side: it rises out of stillness, so the higher flank is a resting level rather than a trough, and the peak scores about half what its neighbours do. One recording lost its opening repetition on exactly that margin — 4.27 against a floor of 5, where every later crossing of the same quadrant scored between 7.2 and 7.8, with the same excursion in the raw brightness and the same fall afterwards.

The bench had made this worse, incidentally. Lengthening the hold before each test made that first peak properly one-sided, where it used to have the start of the movement beside it.

The floor was also doing less work than when it was set. It had been raised to five when prominence was the only test of amplitude the detector had, and camera shake was measured scoring as high as thirteen, so it was never what rejected shake. A peak must now also be one the movement came back from, and that is the test that does.

Four, on measurement. Replayed over the fifty-three recordings made on the current bench, it recovered two counts and lost none, and nothing ran away — the worst count anywhere was a hundred and three, through fifteen seconds of a deliberately static scene at the end of every recording. Three gained nothing further, so four keeps the wider margin.

Twelve recordings were excluded from that sweep, and the exclusion is the part worth copying. They were made by an older bench that did not hold the figure still before a test, so their first peak is not one-sided and the question being asked does not arise for them. Leaving them in would have diluted the result with cases that could not move either way.

The counting screen shows a badge saying how far the movement stands above the background, as a multiple of the threshold it has to clear. It is a useful thing to show someone propping up a phone: it is the difference between "this will not work" and "move nothing, you are fine".

The boundaries between its three bands came, originally, from eight readings on four phones. They were then re-derived by replaying the detector over four hundred and fifty-two recorded bench tests of a hundred repetitions each, and reading the margin each would have shown against what the phone actually counted.

The lower boundary rests on far less, and the code says so where the constants are: 23 of the 452 sat below a margin of two and only 7 below one and a half, so where counting stops is known much less precisely than where it starts.

There is a second-order trap in this that is easy to walk into. The margin is computed by dividing a peak's prominence by the detector's fixed floor, so lowering that floor raises every margin by the same proportion. Had the bands been stored as multiples of the floor, the change described two sections ago would have silently re-graded every movement on every screen — and the badge would then have been reporting a detector threshold rather than the signal. The bands are therefore stored in prominence units, eight and six, and converted against whatever the floor currently is.

The badge is also, deliberately, not called confidence or quality. It says whether the movement is strong enough to be counted and nothing about whether what is being counted is exercise: on two of four phones, a camera shaken by typing on the same desk scored above four here while counting nothing real.

The change that was measured and not kept

One camera in the set has a quadrant whose brightness rises and falls twice for every repetition. Measured against the fundamental, the energy at twice the cadence runs from one and a half to four times larger on that handset, where the phones that count perfectly sit between a twentieth and a half. The extra peak lands halfway between two real ones, and nothing about its height, prominence or shape distinguishes it. Only its timing does.

So: refuse a candidate that arrives sooner than seven tenths of the interval the channel is currently keeping. Seven tenths rather than eight because exercise does not keep time — on a recording that counts perfectly, the intervals run from 0.83 to 1.42 of their median, and a threshold at eight tenths clips that fast tail and cost sixty-six recordings when it was tried. The unwanted peak lands at a half, comfortably clear of both.

The expectation has to come from the peaks the channel is showing, never from the ones already accepted, and that was wrong the first time round: an expectation built from acceptances rises whenever one is refused, which raises the threshold that refused it, and the count walks itself down to every other repetition — measured at 23 of 37 on a recording with nothing whatever wrong with it.

Replayed over a hundred and five recordings from six handsets, the finished rule removed five over-counts, including the 102 on the noisy camera, and took counts above a hundred from nine to five. It also cost nine recordings a repetition apiece, every one of them on a handset with no doubling to remove, so the number counted exactly right slipped from seventy-five to seventy-one.

It was reverted. Five over-counts removed, nine recordings each losing a repetition, and four fewer counted exactly right is not an improvement; it is a redistribution, and the pool said so clearly enough that no argument was needed. The commit that made the change had said as much before it was taken: that trade is the reason to try it on a real suite rather than assume it.

There is a postscript. The experiment then reached production by accident, staged wholesale rather than by path, riding along with the commit before it. It was taken back out in a commit that says what happened in plain words: it was never validated well enough to ship, and shipping it silently is worse than shipping it deliberately. The working tree kept the experiment; the detector went back to what it was.

What the recordings cannot settle

Four hundred and fifty-two recordings is a great deal of evidence about one thing: a phone on a tripod watching an animated figure on a monitor, under a bench rig's own grey backgrounds, at a cadence the rig chose. Ten of the fifteen numbers in DetectorSettings are still the value somebody reasoned their way to. Of the five that are not, one is a reasoned value the recordings moved, and the others are tests that did not exist at all until a recording showed why they had to — which is about the most that can be said for what the remaining ten are worth.

What the bench pool does not contain: a person, a bed, daylight, and a limb that is not a disc. The four traces running under the count on the phone are the same four quadrant signals the bench produces — and they are not all the same shape.

The recording above is a side leg raise, counted by an Oppo handset with the dashboard beside it, and it is the kind of input the bench pool cannot manufacture. Look at the four traces on the phone's screen: one quadrant shows sharp narrow spikes, one shows broad rounded cycles, one shows small ragged ones with more than one bump inside each. That is a single movement, seen four ways, and the detector has to pick two of those four that agree.

So the honest statement of where this got to is not that the counter is exact. It is that above a margin of two — where 429 of those 452 recordings sat — a hundred repetitions come back within two of the truth 93% of the time and exactly right 71% of the time, and that those figures are measured rather than hoped for. Below that margin, far less is known, and the code says so.

What the exercise actually produced was not a set of better numbers. It was a way of asking. The recordings are kept, the settings record stays in one piece, and the failures found this way have synthetic reproductions in the test suite so that they cannot come back unnoticed — the eighty-four samples plotted above are in the repository for exactly that reason, as the fixture of a phantom that will not be counted again. The replay tooling itself is not in the repository at all, and that is the right way round: the durable artefacts are the evidence, the one place the parameters live, and the tests that remember what each number was for.

That is the engineering of it. A camera, four numbers ten times a second, a filter anyone could write, and fifteen constants — most of the work, and all of the interesting part, was in finding out which of those constants were lies.

Automated Testing

A test needs to know the right answer.

That reads as a triviality until the thing being tested is a detector pointed at the physical world. A phone watches an exercise machine for two minutes and reports forty-one repetitions. Were there forty-one? You were the one exercising, and you lost count somewhere in the thirties. Was the lighting representative? Was the camera framed the way it would be in a gym? Was the machine turning at a steady rate, or did you slow down when the phone beeped?

Three things are uncertain at once, and nothing in the system can tell you which of them moved. That is not a test. It is an anecdote with a number in it.

Everything the detector's thresholds were originally set against was one of two kinds of evidence: a synthetic signal invented to match a theory about what exercise looks like, or a mechanical counter clicked by hand. Both turned out to be wrong, repeatedly and in different directions. What follows is what replaced them — three layers of testing, each able to answer a question the one below it cannot.

The tests that need no world

The bottom layer is ordinary unit testing, and there is a lot of it: thirty-four MUnit suites for the browser application and eighteen for the server, plus small sets for the optional diagnostic plugin and the invitation tool. Five hundred and forty-eight tests in all, and a full run takes three and a half minutes on a laptop with no camera, no network and no Google account attached to it.

Nearly three of those minutes are one suite. Forty-six tests push synthetic signals — thousands of samples each — through the real detector, and they are the only part of the run that is not close to instantaneous. As a map of where the risk in this application lives, that is a fair one.

On the detector's side they feed synthetic signals through the real code: the band-pass filter against known responses, peak detection by prominence and spacing, the per-quadrant tallies and the rule that decides which of them to believe, the rest phase, the signal-strength bands. On the server's side they cover configuration, the discovery and isolation of plugins, capability resolution, access policies, the account key, the idle rule, and the validation of everything that arrives from a client.

Two things about them are worth more than the coverage figure.

The first is that the test names are sentences, and the suite reads as a specification of the thing rather than a list of its methods. A report that is not a rep count is rejected rather than guessed at. Recording ids are opaque and unguessable, so one session's captures cannot be enumerated. A channel that has only just begun agreeing cannot freeze the count. Six months later, the only person who will ever read this code needs to recover what a rule was for, and the test that enforces it is a better place to keep that than a comment beside it.

The second is that most of the detector's tests are not inventions. They are regressions, each one built from something that actually went wrong out in the world, with the failure recorded in the test beside the assertion. A quadrant that the moving figure barely reaches has almost no signal, so its threshold falls to the floor and the noise it finds can match the cadence by chance: on a real recording such a channel counted a hundred and thirty-six where a hundred repetitions were performed. There is now a test that manufactures exactly that situation and insists the count stays honest. The margin by which two quadrants of the same movement are allowed to disagree is not a number chosen for comfort; across sixty-five recordings they differed by one repetition, and at most two, and the test says so.

There is an honest limit to this layer, and it is worth stating plainly: these tests do not call Google and do not touch a live Firestore. The queries themselves are exercised only by running the application.

A known truth, manufactured

The second layer is a bench rig, and the idea behind it is simple enough to state in one sentence: if nobody can tell you how many repetitions really happened, then build something that knows.

The bench is a dashboard that also produces the movement being counted. It runs on a desktop screen, animating a figure at a cadence it has chosen itself, with the counting phone on a tripod in front of it. To the phone, the bench is an ordinary dashboard — it arrives over the same direct link described in the previous article, watches the same readings, and sends the same commands. It is part of the shipped application, offered only when the address bar ends in #test.

Because the bench drew the movement, it knows exactly how many cycles have been completed at every instant. The repetition count is exact by construction. And because it is also watching the counter's readings arrive, it can compare the two continuously rather than only at the end.

The rig mid-suite. On the monitor, the bench: a bar pivoting about the bottom-left quadrant with a weight on its free end, four green crosses marking the quadrant centres, and a scoreboard showing what has been shown, what has been counted, and how far off each finished test was. On the phone, the Counter — seeing nothing but that screen.

What the animation has to get right

Drawing a moving shape is easy. Drawing one that stands in for exercise, without accidentally testing something else, took several attempts.

There are three figures. A disc whose centre travels a circle, crossing every quadrant once per cycle. A bar pivoting about the centre of the bottom-left quadrant, its free end swinging through a quarter turn with a disc on the end of it — a forearm and a dumbbell. And a square shuttling between two quadrant centres and back.

The bar sweeps an arc rather than sliding along the chord between its endpoints, and the reason is instructive. Interpolating between the two ends would shorten the bar to seven tenths of its length at mid-swing and grow it back again: a change in size that the exercise it stands in for does not have, and one the detector would certainly see. The same instinct applies to the canvas. The trajectories live on a square field centred inside whatever shape the canvas turns out to be, because scaling horizontally and vertically by different amounts would turn the circle into an ellipse — which is one layout accident away, and was one.

Contrast is the single property that counting has been observed to depend on, so it is a property of a test rather than a detail of the page. The greys come from two bands — 32 to 63 and 192 to 221, with a hundred and twenty-eight levels of daylight between them — and each region is filled with a 45-degree gradient running from the bottom of its band to the top, so a test spans its band rather than sitting at one flat tone. A suite is therefore six tests: three figures, each run once as a light object on a dark ground and once as a dark object on a light one.

Then there are the four small green crosses. They are there to aim the camera by: the trajectories are described in quadrants, and quadrants only mean anything if the camera's quadrants are the canvas's. Framing by eye has been the least reliable part of running a suite, and four marks at the exact centres turn that from a judgement into a check. Every detail of them is a decision. Green, because nothing else on the canvas is ever coloured and they must not be mistaken for part of a test. A mid green rather than a bright one, so they do not become the brightest thing in frame and skew the exposure the camera settles on. Static, so they contribute nothing to a band-passed signal. Drawn underneath the figure rather than over it. And a third of the size they started at, because at the original size the object passing across one covered and uncovered enough of it to be a signal in its own right.

The clock that must not be wrong

A metronomic movement is the easy case, and the detector had already been shown to handle it. Real exercise wanders, so the bench wanders on purpose: the cadence averages 0.8 Hz and is modulated by a slower oscillation that takes the instantaneous frequency between 0.6 and 1.0 Hz, swinging the full way and back every twenty seconds. The low end is chosen deliberately close to the band-pass filter's lower corner of 0.5 Hz — any slower and the harness would be attenuating the movement rather than testing the counter.

That decision has a consequence most of the rig's design hangs from. With a frequency that varies, the number of cycles completed by a given moment is the integral of the instantaneous frequency, not the frequency multiplied by the time. The two differ. Taking the easy one would put the reference count quietly out of step with what is actually on the screen — and the difference would then be reported as the detector's error rather than the harness's.

An oracle that is wrong does not produce no information. It produces confident misinformation, attributed to the thing you were trying to measure.

For the same reason, the comparison is made in seconds rather than in repetitions. Being one short means something quite different at different speeds: at half a hertz it is two seconds of being wrong, and at two hertz it is half of one. A tolerance expressed in repetitions would be strict in the slow tests and lax in the fast ones. So the bench finds the moment the reference passed the count the detector is reporting — by bisection, since there is no closed form once the frequency varies — and reports the lag in seconds. One threshold, two seconds, meaning the same thing everywhere.

A test is a short play in five acts

A test is not "animate a hundred repetitions and compare". Every boundary in the sequence below exists because running them together produced a wrong answer.

StageLengthWhy it is there
Poised3 s A set begins with the weight picked up and held. Starting from an empty frame made the figure's arrival a step change in whichever quadrants it landed in — at the input, indistinguishable from the first half of a repetition, and with no trough before it to be measured against.
Running125 s A hundred repetitions at an average of 0.8 Hz. A hundred rather than thirty: at thirty, about half the tests failed and a different half each run, which is the signature of something intermittent rather than of a figure the detector cannot see.
Holding3 s The movement is over but the weight has not been put down. The last repetition's own return then happens in clear air, instead of tangled up with the step of the object leaving.
Pausing15 s The figure leaves the frame, which is what gives the final peak the trough that every other peak had. The detector confirms a peak from the samples that follow it, so the last repetitions of a set arrive after the movement has stopped.
Settling1.5 s The reset travels to the counting device and takes effect there. Starting to move before it has would credit the first repetitions of a new test to the tail of the last one.

Two of those are worth unpacking, because the numbers in them were measured rather than guessed.

The still moment at the start was one second before it was three. One second was not enough separation: on real recordings the transient from the figure's arrival still covered the first crossing of whichever quadrant it happened to land closest to. Sixteen samples of separation were swallowed; nineteen survived. The quadrant that lost its opening crossing then led the count, and the whole suite came in one short. Three seconds puts the first crossing some thirty samples clear, at a cost of twelve seconds across a suite of six.

Ending a test by making the figure vanish mid-swing, meanwhile, left the last peak one-sided — worth half the prominence of its neighbours — and it went uncounted until the detector's own threshold decayed enough to admit it. Measured on real recordings: twenty seconds on one test, thirty on another.

The arithmetic of all this is itself asserted in a unit test. A standard suite takes 898.5 seconds — about a quarter of an hour — and the test exists so that whoever starts a run knows they are committing to it, rather than discovering the length halfway through.

Watching all the way through

The bench compares the counter against the reference on every animation frame, not at the end of each test. Events are recorded on the edges — the moment the counter falls outside tolerance, the moment it comes back — because sixty identical lines a second would bury the moment it went wrong, which is the thing being looked for.

The most valuable of those watchers tracks stalls. A counter that stops counting and then starts again is a different animal from one that is simply wrong, and when it recovers there are three possibilities worth telling apart: the repetitions performed during the stall are eventually credited, they are silently lost, or they are made up for with interest. The bench records how long the stall lasted, how many repetitions really happened during it, how many were credited, and the shortfall between them.

That watcher earns its keep in a way the final total never could. One recorded test counted nothing for two full minutes while continuing to report the previous test's total — and passed. Its final number was right. Every check that looks only at the end of a test calls that a pass, and the detector that produced it would have gone on being considered correct.

The same suspicion applies to the commands the bench sends. A reset travels over the direct link to the counting device, and that link drops — a phone locks its screen, a network changes underneath it. Sent once, blind, the reset was sometimes lost. It is now sent up to three times over four and a half seconds, and a test that still has no acknowledgement from the far end is recorded as unconfirmed rather than quietly trusted.

The harness is tested too

The bench has its own unit suites, and they are not a formality. The phase never runs backwards, whatever the swing. The time a rep finishes agrees with the count at that time. The bar keeps its length throughout the swing. The movement stays inside the band the detector passes, at every point of its swing. A gradient stays at 45 degrees across a box that is not square.

The stage machine is a pure function, separated from the view and tested on its own, for a specific reason: both of the bench's own bugs have been in it. Scoring a test schedules the next one a second and a half later, and a stage that stayed where it was in the meantime found the pause over again on every frame — so it scored the same test ninety times and sent ninety resets to the other device. A test is scored exactly once, however many frames find the pause over is now an assertion rather than an assumption.

If the oracle is wrong, you are not testing the detector. You are measuring the harness and publishing the result under the detector's name.

What a run leaves behind

A suite produces two kinds of evidence, and both outlive the run.

The first is the stream of observations: test started, reset unconfirmed, discrepancy, stalled, recovered, movement finished, passed, failed, suite finished. Each one carries the run it belongs to, the test's name, the reference count, the acquired count, the lag in seconds and how far into the test it happened. They are filed on their own rather than under anybody's workout, because a run is about the detector's behaviour rather than about anyone's exercise, and the comparisons worth making are between runs.

Each event also carries the handset. That field is chosen by hand at the bench, from a list of names the operator gave the devices themselves, because nothing can be read from the browser instead: the user-agent string names an engine and an operating system, Chrome reports every Android model as "K", and nothing in it says which of a phone's cameras was opened. It earns its place. Runs of the same suite, from the same account, on different handsets have come back exactly right, one short on every test, and fifteen short — and without that field the only way to tell those apart afterwards was to ask whoever ran them what had been on the desk at the time.

The second kind of evidence is the recordings. At the end of each test, during the pause, the bench asks the counting device to capture its signal buffer — the four quadrant brightness traces, as they were, at ten samples a second. The ordering there is load-bearing: the reset that prepares the next test also clears the buffer, so the capture is asked for first and given three seconds to reach the server, because a lost recording costs the entire test that produced it.

A run that goes wrong can be abandoned, which deletes the events it filed and the recordings it asked for, and deliberately leaves the workout alone — that belongs to the account rather than to the run.

Driving the real browser

The third automated layer is an end-to-end suite that drives an actual Chrome through Selenium against the real, running application, rather than calling backend code directly. It lives in its own project, and the single command that runs it is less a test invocation than a piece of defensive choreography: launch a headless Chrome and immediately quit it, so a broken install fails fast with a clear message instead of confusingly inside the first test; check that the application's port is not already taken, by a leftover container from manual testing, say, so the suite cannot silently exercise the wrong process; start the server in the background under coverage instrumentation; poll it until it answers; run the tests; and then stop it by walking the entire process tree, because the server is started through a nested build process that forks a JVM of its own, and stopping only the parent would leave that JVM alive and still holding the port.

What it then asserts is deliberately modest, and the reason is a boundary outside the project's control. Google actively blocks sign-in from WebDriver-controlled browsers — the interactive form specifically, as an anti-automation measure — and there is no emulator to stand in for it, because Google Cloud's emulators cover databases and messaging, while the Firebase Authentication emulator only intercepts calls made through the Firebase SDK and this application speaks OAuth 2.0 directly.

So signing in is done by hand, once. A build task leaves three things running: the local Firestore emulator, a coverage-instrumented server pointed at it, and a visible Chrome with remote debugging enabled on a profile of its own. You sign in there and leave the window open; a later run attaches to that same browser over the DevTools protocol instead of launching a fresh one, and walks the signed-in application — opening About and checking it reports the account and every build field, then stepping into the dashboard and the counter and back out. The emulator keeps everything in memory, so it has to stay up for the whole interval between the sign-in and the run.

All of this traffic is instrumented by the same coverage recorder as the unit tests, and the measurements accumulate rather than overwrite, so one report at the end can reflect every layer that was run.

What the rig cannot do

A desktop screen is an honest movement and a dishonest scene. It is flat, evenly lit, free of reflections, and it never moves. Everything that makes a real room difficult is absent from it by construction, which is exactly why the rig can measure what it measures — and exactly what it cannot tell you about.

The camera is the clearest example. A phone camera is an active participant: it meters, it adjusts, and it will happily re-expose a scene in the middle of a set. The application answers that by holding exposure, white balance and focus still once metering has settled, and nearly everything learned about doing so was learned by pointing real handsets at real things. Switching a control to manual puts some Android cameras into a state where the picture goes dark. Asking for a fixed shutter without also fixing the sensitivity leaves the sensitivity wherever the driver puts it — as low as 21 on a camera that was metering at 100, which is a picture four stops darker than the one it replaced. And an attempt can report success and still be wrong: the controls were held, and held at the wrong value. "Held" alone says nothing about whether the picture is usable, which is why the outcome now records what the camera reported afterwards, verbatim.

Two things the rig cannot automate. Which handset is on the tripod — the bench's own list names twenty-three of them, front camera and back — and a hand reaching in to set exposure, sensitivity, colour and focus by hand when the automatic choice is wrong.

Framing is the second. The crosses make the alignment checkable, but somebody still has to do the checking, and a suite framed a little wrong is a suite whose quadrants are not the canvas's.

The third is the breadth of devices, and it is the one the recorded numbers keep pointing at. The same suite, the same account, three handsets, three different answers. No amount of animation fixes that; it is answered by owning a drawer full of phones and running the suite on all of them.

And the fourth is the actual subject. The rig proves that the detector counts a known movement correctly. It says nothing about whether the thing in front of somebody's phone in a real room produces a signal of the kind the rig has been feeding it — and a real room has reflective metal in it, and a window behind the machine, and movements with more than one event in every cycle. That question is answered by pointing real phones at real machines, and the recordings that come back from doing so are the subject of the article that follows.

What comes next

The rig does not tune anything. It produces evidence: a few hundred scored tests, each with a known truth, a recorded answer, a named handset, and a minute of the four raw signals that produced it.

The last article in this series is about what happened when every tuning decision in the detector was replayed against that evidence, offline, as many times as a question needed asking. The prominence floor, the window length, the tolerance, the corroboration margin and the signal-strength bands were all first chosen by reasoning about signals nobody had recorded, and every guess made that way turned out to be wrong in a different direction. The recordings are the only reason anybody could tell which.

Practical Usage

The four articles before this one were about how the counting works. This one is about the part the counting is in service of, which is a number of kilocalories — where it comes from, why it is not a measurement of you, and how to make it mean something for the particular piece of equipment in your particular room.

The short version: there is one formula, it has four terms, and one of those terms is a setting you are expected to calibrate yourself. Most of what follows is about that one term.

Pointing it at the machine

None of the arithmetic matters until something is being counted correctly, so the practical part first.

Sign in on a phone and it opens the Counter and asks for the camera. Prop it where the moving part of the exercise fills a good share of the picture — not the whole body, not the whole room, the part that moves. For the first fifteen seconds or so nothing appears: the detector is working out whether there is a cadence there at all. Then the count arrives, including the repetitions performed while it was deciding.

The badge beside the status line is the thing to look at while you are still adjusting the phone. It says how far the movement stands out from its background, as a multiple of the threshold a repetition has to clear. Green counts reliably. Amber is marginal. Red will miss repetitions. If it is not green, move the phone rather than hoping — and the ↺ control starts the count over, which is how you throw away the movement of climbing onto the machine.

To see the figures on something other than the phone that is counting, sign in on a tablet and choose Dashboard. The two connect directly to each other and must be on the same network. A workout begins only at Start — signing in does not begin one, and neither does taking over the counting — and ends at Stop, at sign-out, or ten minutes after the counter last reported.

The screen, and where every number on it comes from

Seventy repetitions, 61.3 kilocalories, one minute and thirty-three seconds in. The header says which exercise's settings produced it: factor 1.25, counting by rep count.

Two numbers on that screen came from the phone — the count, and the clock. Everything else was worked out by the tablet from those two and the account's settings, and all of it can be checked by hand.

At a weight of 70 kg — the starting value, and what the settings panel further down shows — with the factor at 1.25 and counting by repetitions, the calorie figure is 70 × 70 × 1.25 ÷ 100 = 61.25, which the screen rounds to 61.3. That is the whole calculation. No model, no lookup table, no heart rate.

The two rates are measured over the last ten repetitions rather than over the whole session, so that watching mid-set tells you how fast you are going now rather than how fast you have been going on average. The two projections add the remainder: the total so far, plus that rate applied to the time left before the next half-hour mark. At 1:33 there are twenty-eight and a half minutes still to run, so 52.2 repetitions a minute reaches about 1,554 and 45.7 kilocalories a minute about 1,360 — which is what the screen says, to within the rounding on the rates themselves.

The calorie figure is also, plainly, nonsense. Forty-six kilocalories a minute is roughly three times what half an hour at top sustainable effort produces for a 70 kg adult. Nothing is broken: the factor is still the shipped default, which was calibrated for the frequency formula and is here being applied to a rep count. An uncalibrated factor is exactly what that looks like, and the rest of this article is how to fix it.

The whole formula

calories = measure × weight in kg × factor ÷ 100
counting by rep count: measure = the number of repetitions
counting by frequency: measure = the sum of every repetition's own rate, in reps per second
the 100 exists only to put the factor on a scale a person can read and nudge

The weight is yours and is kept once for the whole account. The measure is what the counter reported. The factor is the number you set per exercise, and it is the only place in the formula where the particular machine, its resistance setting, your technique and your range of movement are allowed to live.

The hundred deserves a word, because it is pure ergonomics. A factor calibrated against a real machine lands on values like 0.0125, which is correct, unreadable and impossible to adjust by eye — three leading zeros away from telling you whether one exercise is harder than another. Dividing by a hundred moves the same calibration to 1.25, where 1.25 and 1.30 are visibly different numbers and a step of 0.05 is a real adjustment. It changes nothing about the arithmetic.

The frequency measure is the one that needs explaining. Every repetition from the second onwards contributes the reciprocal of the gap since the one before it. A repetition a second apart adds 1.0. A repetition half a second apart adds 2.0. Over a set this sum grows with the number of repetitions and with how fast they came, which means it grows with the square of the cadence — go twice as fast for the same length of time and you do twice as many repetitions, each worth twice as much, for four times the total.

Why there are two of them

They are not two approximations of the same thing, and the choice is not a matter of taste.

Counting repetitions says every repetition costs the same. That is true of a weight lifted the same way each time: a dumbbell curl with 12 kg moves 12 kg through the same arc whether you take two seconds over it or four, so the mechanical work is identical and the energy cost very nearly so. Doing them faster gets you through the set sooner; it does not make each one harder.

Counting frequency says the opposite — that the whole of the effort is in how fast the thing is going round. That is true of a bicycle, a rowing machine, a stair climber. On a stepper set to a fixed resistance, the pedal travel is the same every step; what changes between a stroll and a hard effort is purely the rate, and the energy cost rises much faster than the rate does.

Pick the one that describes what actually varies when you push harder. If pushing harder means more repetitions, count repetitions. If pushing harder means the same repetitions, faster, count frequency.

What the factor is attached to

The factor belongs to the equipment, not to the exercise's name. "Stationary bicycle" costs quite different amounts of effort on two machines set to two resistances, and the factor is where that difference lives — which is why an exercise in the settings carries a free-text equipment line as well as a name. Two entries called "Chest press" with different machines and different factors are the correct way to use this, not a mistake.

A factor is per exercise. There is also a default factor, used by a new exercise until you give it one of its own, and it ships at 1.25 — a value calibrated against a real machine rather than a placeholder, but calibrated against someone else's real machine, which makes it a starting point and nothing more.

One more property matters for what comes later: when a workout is stopped from a dashboard, the exercise, the factor, the weight and the counting mode are frozen into that history row along with the calorie total. Change your factor next week and last week's workouts keep the numbers they were recorded with. The raw measurement — repetitions, the frequency sum, the duration — is kept beside the snapshot, so an old workout can be recalculated under a new factor by anybody willing to do the multiplication.

Calibrating it: five minutes and a lookup

The factor cannot be derived from first principles, because it encodes things nobody has measured: the resistance the machine is set to, how far you actually move, how efficiently you do it. What it can be is anchored to published figures for the same activity, which is the method that follows.

One. Pick the exercise in the settings, choose its counting mode, and leave the factor wherever it is. Start a workout and do five minutes at the hardest intensity you can hold for five minutes. Not a sprint, and not a warm-up — the intensity you would describe to somebody as working hard.

Two. Stop the workout. Open History from the dashboard menu and export the CSV. The row you just made carries duration_seconds, reps and cadence_sum, which are the raw measurement, untouched by whatever the factor happened to be.

Three. Look up what that activity is generally reckoned to cost, in METs. The standard reference is the Compendium of Physical Activities, which lists an activity once per intensity, so what you get is a range rather than a number.

Four. Place yourself in that range honestly. This is the step that feels unscientific and is not: nobody else can do it for you, and a range exists precisely because the activity is done at different intensities by different people. If you have been doing this exercise for years and can go faster and longer than other people who use the same equipment, the top of the range is a fair description of you. If you took it up last month, it is not. Pick a percentage and write down why.

Five. Convert METs to kilocalories per minute, by the usual formula — METs × 3.5 × your weight in kg ÷ 200 — and multiply by five.

Six. Solve the formula backwards for the factor:

factor = kilocalories × 100 ÷ (measure × weight in kg)
measure is the reps column, or the cadence_sum column, depending on the mode

Type the result into the factor field. It takes a typed value, not only the nudge buttons, which exists for exactly this reason.

Worked example: a stair climber, counted by frequency

A stepper is a frequency exercise: the resistance does not change, only how fast you go. Say an 80 kg exerciser who has used this machine for years.

The five-minute set comes back as 300 repetitions over 300 seconds — about sixty steps a minute — and the CSV gives a cadence_sum of 298.0. (At one step a second the frequency sum lands very close to the rep count, which is a useful sanity check that you have read the right column.)

The Compendium lists stair climbing at 4.5 METs for a slow pace and 9.3 for a fast one, one step at a time. Our exerciser puts themselves at 75% of that range, on the grounds of being demonstrably faster and longer-lasting than other people on the same machine: 4.5 + 0.75 × 4.8 = 8.1 METs.

The frequency calibration. The measure is the summed rate, not the rep count, which is why the factor comes out well below one.

Type 0.24 into the factor field for that exercise and the dashboard will report 56.7 kilocalories for a set exactly like the calibration set — because that is what you just told it to do.

Worked example: dumbbell curls, counted by repetitions

The same method, the other mode. Twelve-kilogram dumbbells, the same 80 kg exerciser.

Five minutes of curls is not five minutes of continuous movement, and that is fine — the clock runs through the rests on purpose, and the published figures for resistance training include the rests too. Say the set comes back as 60 repetitions over five minutes.

The Compendium's resistance-training entries run from 3.5 METs for multiple exercises at varied resistance to 6.0 for vigorous power lifting or body building. At 75% of that range: 3.5 + 0.75 × 2.5 = 5.375 METs.

Those two factors are not comparable with each other, and that is not a defect. 0.24 and 0.78 are answers to different questions: one is the cost of a unit of cadence, the other the cost of a repetition. A factor is only meaningful beside the mode it was calibrated in, which is why the dashboard header prints both — "factor 1.25 · rep count" — rather than the number alone.

What changing pace does to each

Here is why the choice of mode matters more than the choice of factor.

Take the stair climber calibration above and, for the sake of the comparison, calibrate the same five-minute set both ways: 56.7 kilocalories from 300 repetitions gives a rep-count factor of 0.24 as well. At the calibration pace, the two modes now agree exactly. Then change the pace.

The same five minutes, the same 80 kg, both modes calibrated to agree at sixty repetitions a minute. Rep count is a straight line through the origin; frequency is a parabola. At ninety a minute the two differ by half as much again.

Do the same five minutes 20% faster — 360 repetitions instead of 300 — and rep count reports 68.0 kilocalories, which is 20% more. Frequency reports 81.7, which is 44% more, because 1.2 squared is 1.44. Go 50% faster and rep count reports half as much again while frequency reports two and a quarter times as much.

Neither is wrong. They are claims about different machines. If your stepper really does cost four times as much at twice the rate, the parabola is the honest curve and the straight line is undercounting you badly at the top end. If your dumbbell really does cost the same per curl however fast you do it, the straight line is honest and the parabola will reward you handsomely for rushing your form. Choosing the mode wrong is a much larger error than getting the factor slightly wrong, and no amount of recalibration fixes it — it only moves the point where the two curves happen to cross.

Reading it back out, and changing your mind

Where the three settings in the formula live. Weight is kept once for the account; every exercise has its own counting mode and its own factor.

History lists every workout newest first, and its menu exports the lot as a CSV file. The columns are the measurement rather than the application's internals: start, end, exercise, duration, repetitions, cadence sum, weight, factor, counting mode, calories. The calorie figure is written out beside everything it was computed from, on purpose — a file that gave you only the total would be a file you could not check and could not recalculate.

That matters most when you decide the calibration was wrong. A factor is a judgement about yourself made on one afternoon, and the honest way to improve it is to wear a heart-rate monitor for a session, compare what it reports with what the dashboard does, and scale the factor by the ratio. Doing that does not retroactively change anything already in the history — but every row in the export still carries the raw measurement, so you can multiply the old sets by the new factor in a spreadsheet and see what the correction does to a month of workouts.

What the number is not

It is not a measurement of your metabolism. Nothing in this system observes you: there is no heart rate, no oxygen, no body composition. There is a count of repetitions, a weight you typed in, and a constant you chose. The output is a kilocalorie only in so far as the factor has been calibrated to make it one, which is the entire reason the factor is a setting rather than a constant in the source.

The published figures the calibration is anchored to carry their own uncertainty. The MET itself is defined as 3.5 ml of oxygen per kilogram per minute, which is a convention rather than a fact about any individual: real resting rates vary with age, sex, body composition and health, so the same activity at the same rate does not cost two people the same. A range drawn from a published compendium is the right order of magnitude and should not be mistaken for better than that.

And the 75% is a judgement, not a measurement. It is the honest kind of judgement — one made explicitly, written down, and attached to a number that can be corrected — but a different person would pick differently, and the resulting calorie figures would differ by as much as the range itself does.

What the number is good for is comparison with itself. The same exercise, on the same machine, at the same factor, on Tuesday and on Friday: that difference is real, because everything uncertain about it is held constant on both sides and cancels out. A figure whose absolute accuracy is only as good as the judgement behind it, but which is consistent to the repetition week over week, still tells you accurately whether you are doing more than you were. That is what a counter on a tripod can honestly offer, and it is most of what anybody wanted from one.

Strings, Dates, and Type Checking

A calculator that only knows numbers is fine for plotting sine waves, but real scratchpad questions rarely stay numeric for long. How many days until a deadline? What timestamp is 36 hours after a release window? How do you pull an order number out of a log line or reformat a date string?

The Formula Builder now speaks strings, UTC dates, and durations, backed by a Hindley-Milner type checker and an expression-aware variable editor.

Strings and regular expressions

The Strings tab starts with a string constant block whose double quotes sit outside the editable text box, so you type the raw text directly without fighting quote marks. Next to it are the usual text primitives:

  • length(s), trim(s), capitalize(s), toUpper(s), toLower(s)

  • substring(s, offset, len), append(a, b), times(s, count)

  • toString(x) and toNumber(s) for converting across types

  • find(s, regexp) returning the zero-based index of the first match, or -1 if nothing matches

  • replace(s, regexp, replacement) and the four-argument replace(s, regexp, replacement, flags)

A few quick examples you can paste straight into the Import dialog:

capitalize(trim("  hello world  "))
// => "Hello world"

append(times("ha", 3), "!")
// => "hahaha!"

find("Order #4821 shipped", "[0-9]+")
// => 7

substring("Order #4821 shipped", 7, 4)
// => "4821"

replace supports backreferences in the replacement string: \1 through \9 expand to capture groups, and & expands to the entire match. An optional fourth argument supplies regex flags such as "g" (global) or "gi" (global, case-insensitive):

replace("2026-11-29", "([0-9]{4})-([0-9]{2})-([0-9]{2})", "\3/\2/\1")
// => "29/11/2026"

replace("a=12, b=345", "[0-9]+", "[&]", "g")
// => "a=[12], b=[345]"

replace("Hello hello HELLO", "hello", "hi", "gi")
// => "hi hi hi"

Dates and durations

The Dates tab keeps dates and durations as distinct types, all anchored in UTC so results do not shift depending on which browser timezone opens them.

Dates are built with from(...), dateOf(y, m, d), dateTimeOf(y, m, d, h, min, s), or now(). from accepts ISO-8601 strings, common human-readable date formats, or an integer millisecond epoch (as either a number or a digit string). Invalid calendar dates such as from("2026-02-30") are rejected outright rather than silently rolling over into March:

formatDate(from("2026-11-29"))
// => "2026-11-29T00:00:00.000Z"

formatDate(from("Nov 29, 2026"))
// => "2026-11-29T00:00:00.000Z"

formatDate(from(0))
// => "1970-01-01T00:00:00.000Z"

Subtracting two dates with dateDiff(a, b) produces a (possibly negative) duration, and adding or subtracting a duration with dateAdd(d, dur) or dateSub(d, dur) yields another date. Durations can be constructed with days, hours, minutes, seconds, millis, duration(d, h, m, s, ms), or parseDuration(s), combined with durAdd / durSub / durScale / durDiv / durAbs, and converted to numbers with toDays through toMillis:

formatDuration(dateDiff(from("2026-12-25"), from("2026-11-29")))
// => "26:00:00:00:000"

formatDuration(dateDiff(from("2026-11-29"), from("2026-12-25")))
// => "-26:00:00:00:000"

formatDate(dateAdd(from("2026-11-29T09:30:00Z"), duration(2, 5, 45, 0, 0)))
// => "2026-12-01T15:15:00.000Z"

durDiv(days(7), hours(8))
// => 21

formatDuration(durScale(minutes(45), 4))
// => "0:03:00:00:000"

formatDate(dateAdd(from("2026-11-29T09:30:00Z"), hours(36)), "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm")
// => "2026-11-30 21:30"

formatDate(from("2026-11-29"), "cccc, LLLL d, yyyy")
// => "Sunday, November 29, 2026"

formatDuration(
  dateDiff(from("2026-12-25T12:00:00Z"), from("2026-11-29T09:30:00Z")),
  "d'd' hh'h' mm'm'"
)
// => "26d 02h 30m"

Individual components can be pulled out directly with year, month, day, weekday, hour, minute, second, millisecond, and toEpoch. Dates and durations also support value equality (===, !==) and ordering (<, <=, >, >=).

Variables are no longer restricted to bare numbers. Any variable can hold a full expression — a parsed date like from("2026-12-31T23:59:59Z"), a string, a duration, or even a function — and every variable row has an edit button (✎) that opens the block workspace for that variable. To keep dependency tracking simple, variable definitions cannot reference other variables.

Behind the scenes, every formula and variable is checked by a Hindley-Milner type inference engine before it runs. Arithmetic operators require numbers, comparisons require matching orderable types, both branches of if/else must agree on a type, and function calls check both arity and argument types. Trying to evaluate "hello" + 1 or from("2026-11-29") + days(1) reports the exact mismatch instead of falling back to JavaScript coercion.

The graph button respects those inferred types too: it stays enabled only when the formula represents a number → number mapping, and disables itself for strings, dates, booleans, or multi-argument functions.

Recursion with λ and μ

Once a calculator has conditional expressions, anonymous functions, and a fixed-point operator, it stops being just an expression evaluator and becomes a small functional programming language — without adding loops, mutable state, or statements.

The λ/μ tab adds four building blocks to the Formula Builder:

  • lambda(x, body) (or lambda(a, b, body) for multiple parameters) builds an anonymous function.

  • mu(fn) computes the fixed point of a function whose first parameter is a reference to itself, enabling recursion.

  • param refers to a bound lambda parameter (kept distinct from global variables).

  • f(x), f(x, y), and f(x, y, z) apply a function value to arguments.

Because lambdas and mu are first-class values, they work identically over numbers, booleans, strings, and dates, and they can be stored in variables via either the text box or the graphic block editor.

Factorial with μ and λ

Start with the textbook recursive function:

mu(lambda(f, x, x <= 0 ? 1 : x * f(x - 1)))

Read it from the inside out:

  1. lambda(f, x, ...) defines a two-argument function that takes the recursive function itself as f and the input number as x.

  2. When x <= 0, it returns the base case 1; otherwise it multiplies x by f(x - 1).

  3. Wrapping that lambda in mu(...) ties the knot: mu hands back a one-argument function of type number → number where every call to f re-enters the same function.

You can invoke it directly:

(mu(lambda(f, x, x <= 0 ? 1 : x * f(x - 1))))(6)
// => 720

Or store mu(lambda(f, x, x <= 0 ? 1 : x * f(x - 1))) in a variable named fact and call fact(10) (3628800) in the main formula. Because the inferred type of fact is number → number, the Graph button stays active if you want to plot fact(x) over small values of x. (A recursion depth guard stops runaway recursion cleanly if a base case is missed.)

Testing primality with a recursive helper

You can nest mu inside an outer lambda to hide internal loop state. A trial-division primality test checks the edge cases up front (anything less than 2, non-integers, 2, and even numbers), then hands off to a recursive helper that walks odd divisors 3, 5, 7, ... until d * d > n:

lambda(n,
  n < 2 || n !== floor(n) ? false :
  n === 2 ? true :
  n % 2 === 0 ? false :
  mu(lambda(check, d,
    d * d > n ? true :
    n % d === 0 ? false :
    check(d + 2)
  ))(3)
)

Store that expression in a variable isPrime. The type checker infers number → boolean (and automatically disables the Graph button, since the result is a boolean rather than a number). Calling isPrime(N) gives:

isPrime(1)   // => false
isPrime(2)   // => true
isPrime(97)  // => true
isPrime(99)  // => false
isPrime(997) // => true

Counting change in Singapore coins (using a string as an array)

The Formula Builder deliberately has no list or array type — only numbers, booleans, strings, dates, durations, and functions. Yet strings plus find, substring, length, and toNumber are all you need to represent a list of numbers as a comma-separated string such as "50,20,10,5" (the four circulating Singapore coin denominations in cents: 50¢, 20¢, 10¢, and 5¢).

Given a non-empty string coins:

  • Find the first separator with comma = find(coins, ",").

  • The head coin is toNumber(comma === -1 ? coins : substring(coins, 0, comma)).

  • The tail of remaining coins is comma === -1 ? "" : substring(coins, comma + 1, length(coins) - comma - 1).

To avoid computing find(coins, ",") four times per step, we bind it once with an immediately invoked lambda(comma, ...)(find(coins, ",")) — the standard lambda-calculus encoding of a let binding.

That gives the classic recursive Count Change recurrence — the number of ways to make amount using coins equals the ways that use at least one of the first coin plus the ways that skip the first coin altogether:

mu(lambda(cc, amount, coins,
  amount === 0 ? 1 :
  amount < 0 || length(coins) === 0 ? 0 :
  lambda(comma,
    cc(amount - toNumber(comma === -1 ? coins : substring(coins, 0, comma)), coins)
    + cc(amount, comma === -1 ? "" : substring(coins, comma + 1, length(coins) - comma - 1))
  )(find(coins, ","))
))

The type checker infers (number, string) → number. Save it in a variable cc and evaluate it on Singapore's "50,20,10,5" denominations:

cc(10, "50,20,10,5")  // => 2   (10; 5+5)
cc(20, "50,20,10,5")  // => 4   (20; 10+10; 10+5+5; 5+5+5+5)
cc(50, "50,20,10,5")  // => 13
cc(100, "50,20,10,5") // => 49  ways to make $1.00
cc(200, "50,20,10,5") // => 242 ways to make $2.00

Because the denominations live in a plain string, changing the coin set is a single edit to the argument — no new syntax, no array type, just strings and recursion.
